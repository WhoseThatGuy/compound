Trial Conversion & Brand Audit

01

Executive diagnosis

What's fundamentally right and wrong, before anything else.

What's genuinely working

  • Real photography, no stock. Every facility, coach, and athlete photo is actually Compound. This alone puts it ahead of most local gym sites.
  • Copy discipline. No "transform your life," no countdown timers, no guru language. The restraint is a genuine brand asset — rare in this category.
  • Pricing transparency. No hidden joining fees, plain cancellation terms. Builds trust with a skeptical, training-literate audience.
  • "Included, not upsold." The sauna framing quietly, correctly rejects the amenities-tier upsell that chain gyms run on.
  • The trial itself is low-pressure by design. "No obligation," repeated without irony, and meant.

What's holding it back

  • The hero sells the room before it sells the person. Square meterage and platform counts land before anyone learns whether people like them train here.
  • "Serious training" is under-specified. It reads as strength-gym-generic until proven otherwise — nothing yet tells a first-HYROX beginner they're included in that word.
  • No motion, no sound. The brand wants "energy" and "people pushing themselves." Static photography, however good, can only imply that — it can't show it.
  • Social proof is a number, not a voice. 4.9★ from 233 reviews is real and repeated three times, but never once quoted. The only words on the site are Compound's own.
  • Membership is segmented by clock, not by identity. Off Peak / Standard / Youth speaks to when you train, never to why — which is exactly the range the brand claims to serve.

1. The hero doesn't tell an unsure visitor they belong here
High impactLow effort
Problem

The lede lists activities (runners, lifters, fighters, field athletes) but never explicitly tells a beginner, a HYROX first-timer, or someone returning from injury that they qualify as "serious." "Serious training" reads as a bar to clear, not an invitation.

Why it matters

Per Compound's own positioning, "athlete" is meant to span the full range — but the silent objection ("is this too intense for me?") is exactly the thing stopping an unsure visitor from clicking the trial CTA, and nothing currently answers it.

Recommended change

Make the invitation explicit in the lede, not just the activity list.

2. Social proof is a number, never a voice
High impactLow effort
Problem

"4.9 from 233 reviews" appears on three pages, always followed by a Compound-written paraphrase ("Members consistently point to…"). Not one real sentence written by an actual member appears anywhere on the site.

Why it matters

For a brand built on "no bullshit" and authenticity, a marketing paraphrase pretending to summarise reviews is the opposite of proof — it's the one place the site could let someone other than Compound talk, and doesn't.

Recommended change

Pull 3–4 short, verbatim quotes from the existing 233 reviews (with permission), credited by first name and what they train for — "first HYROX," "back after a knee reco," "used to just avoid the gym." Place them near the trial CTAs, not buried in a dedicated testimonials page. This also does double duty: it shows the beginner-to-competitive range in real voices instead of Compound asserting it.

Expected impact

High — the raw material already exists (233 real reviews); this is a content pull, not new proof to generate.

3. The homepage sells specs before it sells belonging
Medium impactLow effort
Problem

650m², 9 platforms, 24/7 loads directly under the hero CTA. Real, good facts — but facility facts. The "Trusted by Dunedin's top athletes" strip, the closest thing to a belonging signal, is a separate scroll-stop after it.

5. No motion, no sound — a kinetic brand told through still photos
High impactHigher effort
Problem

Every page is static. The brand wants "energy," "people pushing themselves," "a place where you want to train" — that's a kinetic feeling. Photos, even genuinely good ones, can only imply it.

Why it matters

This is the widest gap between "good gym photography" and "you can feel what training here is like" — the exact bar the brand brief sets for its imagery.

Recommended change

A single 15–30 second muted, looping clip in the hero or About section — real members, real coaching, the actual noise of plates and turf implied through motion. Free-trial funnel teardown

Ad, search, or word of mouth → website → decision → form → real-world visit. Where it actually leaks, and where it doesn't.

1

Arrival — homepage or free-pass landing page

Paid traffic likely lands directly on free-pass.html, which is genuinely a good single-purpose landing page: one job, low friction, no distractions. Organic and word-of-mouth traffic more often lands on the homepage first.

If arriving at the homepage first: the "is this too intense for me" ambiguity (Opportunity 1) is live risk here — an unsure visitor can bounce before ever reaching a trial CTA.
2

Deciding to act

The offer itself is clear and genuinely low-pressure — "no obligation," repeated and meant, with no manufactured urgency. This is a real strength; nothing here needs fixing.

3

The form

Now four fields — first name, last name, email, phone — down from six after this project trimmed the message field and interest dropdown that didn't belong on a single-purpose trial page. Friction here is genuinely low.

No material risk remaining at this step.
4

Immediately after submitting

The confirmation is a single vague sentence with no timeframe.

This is where quiet doubt creeps in — "did that actually work?" (Opportunity 6). Cheap to fix, real trust return.
5

Between submitting and walking in

"Get your key-tag: book a setup time, or come in during staffed hours" is functionally fine but generic. The last real anxiety a first-timer has before showing up — "will I feel out of place" — isn't addressed anywhere on the site.

Does the trial feel like an invitation rather than a funnel? Mostly, yes — genuinely one of the site's real strengths. The repeated "no pressure," "no obligation" language, and the total absence of urgency tricks or popups, are doing real work and should not be touched. The leaks that exist are upstream (does an unsure visitor even reach the CTA) and just past conversion (does the confirmation reassure them) — not in the form itself, which is in good shape.

05

Brand assessment

Does this actually feel like Compound? Honestly — mostly.

Yes, more than most local business sites manage. The photography discipline — real, unstaged, named-equipment specific — and the copy restraint — no guru language, no fake urgency, no countdown timers — genuinely set it apart from a generic gym template, and specifically from CityFitness's national-chain voice.

But right now it reads as a tasteful, honest gym more than it reads as a gym with pride and identity. It's stronger on "authentic" and "no bullshit" than it is on "pride," "ambition," and "energy" — the pillars that are hardest to fake and currently least represented. That's a real gap, but a fixable one — the fix is imagery and voice, not more adjectives, which is exactly the trap a site this disciplined could easily fall into if pushed the wrong way.

Reem Kufi's uppercase, geometric headlines paired with Montserrat's clean body text is a good, slightly unusual choice — it avoids both the bold-slab "GYM FONT" cliché and the sterile, over-designed feel the brand explicitly wants to avoid. The navy/olive/beige palette is moody without tipping into luxury-fitness gold-and-marble territory. These are correct calls; don't touch them.

Can someone looking at the site understand what it feels like to train here? Partly. The facility photography answers "what does the room look like" convincingly. It doesn't yet answer "what does it sound and feel like when it's full" — that's the motion gap named in Opportunity 5, and it's the single biggest lever left for making the brand feel as alive as the brief wants it to be.

06

Competitive differentiation

Grounded in CityFitness's actual live homepage, pulled for this review — not assumption.

CityFitness leads with

  • "Memberships from $7.99 per week" — price, first thing on the page
  • "60+ Clubs Nationwide" — scale, repeated as a scrolling city list
  • A live urgency banner: "Now's the best time to join"
  • An amenities checklist: Pilates, Yoga, massage chairs, recovery rooms, "free gym bag"
  • Generic, replaceable-anywhere language: "Achieve More Everyday"

Compound correctly does the opposite

  • Price never appears on the homepage — it's on a dedicated page, where it belongs
  • No scale claims — one room, one city, inside an actual stadium
  • No urgency banners, no manufactured scarcity
  • Sauna framed as "included, not upsold" — the direct rejection of the amenities-tier play
  • Specific, real language: "Eleiko bars," "40m turf sprint lane," "chalk allowed"

The instinct not to compete with CityFitness on its own terms is already correct throughout the site. What's worth leaning into harder, because a 60-club franchise structurally cannot replicate it:

  • The stadium. No other Dunedin gym trains inside Forsyth Barr Stadium. This is a visual and narrative asset that's currently just a detail in the address — it should be a story.
  • 24/7 key-tag access on a single-site gym. Many boutique gyms don't offer this; many chains offer it without the boutique feel. Compound has both — worth stating more plainly as a differentiator, not just a hero stat.
  • A fixed local roster. A franchise's staff and members rotate; Compound's don't. Named coaches, named athletes, real quotes — a chain has no equivalent to offer, because it has no fixed community to draw from.
  • Specificity over policy-safe copy. Chains write copy that has to work in 60 locations, which is why it says nothing. Compound can name the actual bar brand, the actual turf length, the actual coach — because it's one real place. Do more of this, not less.

10

Final verdict

Would I start the trial? Yes — but slower than I should.

As a first-time Dunedin visitor who takes training moderately seriously but isn't a competitive athlete: the trust signals clear the bar. Real photos, transparent pricing, genuinely low-pressure language, and forms that now actually work — nothing here reads scummy or corporate, which is most of the battle against a category full of both.

But I'd hesitate a beat longer than I should, for one specific reason: nothing on the page tells me, directly, that someone like me — not yet fit, first HYROX on the calendar, coming back from a long break — actually belongs here. The site currently assumes I'll infer that from "serious training." Compound's own positioning says that's exactly the ambiguity it shouldn't leave to the visitor to resolve alone.

Fix that one thing — in the hero, in the membership framing, in real voices instead of a rating — and the rest of the site is honestly already doing its job.
